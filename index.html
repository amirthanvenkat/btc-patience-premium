How the signals work

Two measurements of momentum. RSI scores how hard the price has been pushed lately on a scale of 0 to 100, where low means beaten down and high means running hot. Williams %R asks where this week's price sits inside its recent range, from 0 at the very top to −100 at the very bottom. When both agree that things are extreme, that has tended to be a moment worth noticing, though as the panels below note, the two are not equally important at both ends.

A buying signal

RSI below 30
and
Williams %R below −80

Both deeply oversold, meaning the price has fallen hard and is sitting near the bottom of its recent range. In practice RSI is doing the work here: weekly RSI has dropped below 30 only twelve times since 2012, and on every one of those occasions Williams %R was already below −80 nearby. The second condition has never actually excluded anything on the buy side.

A selling signal

RSI above 90
and
Williams %R above −10

Both stretched to an extreme at once, which has only happened near major peaks. Here the second condition genuinely bites: dropping the Williams %R requirement changes the outcome materially, unlike on the buy side. The exact levels differ by preset.

Words you will meet here

Nothing below assumes you already know any of this.

Bitcoin
A digital currency you can buy and hold like a share. Nobody runs it. Its price moves far more than shares do, both up and down.
Weekly chart
Each bar covers a whole week instead of a day. It filters out short term noise, so signals are slower and there are fewer of them.
Dollar cost averaging
Buying a set amount on a regular schedule, say the first of every month, regardless of the price. It removes the need to guess the right moment, which almost nobody manages reliably.
RSI
Relative Strength Index. A 0 to 100 score for how hard the price has been pushed recently. Below 30 is usually called oversold, above 70 overbought. Above 90 on a weekly chart is rare.
Williams %R
A second momentum measure, running from 0 down to −100. Near 0 means the price is closing at the top of its recent range, near −100 means the bottom.
Oversold
A reading saying the price has fallen hard and fast. Historically these have often been good moments to buy, though it never guarantees the fall has finished.
Confluence
Two different measurements agreeing at the same time. Agreement filters out signals that either one alone would give too often.
Multiple
What you ended with divided by what you paid in. Pay in $10,000, end with $15,000, that is 1.5x.
Worst dip
The biggest fall from a high point to a later low along the way. Everything on this page went through large ones, so it is worth knowing before you start rather than discovering later.
Backtest
Running a rule against past prices to see what it would have done. It shows what already happened, which is useful, but it is a record rather than a forecast.
Hindsight
Knowing something only after the fact. Peaks are obvious on an old chart and much harder to call while they are forming, which is why this page always shows when a signal could actually have been acted on.

Keeping it in proportion

Everything above is drawn from a single fourteen year run of one asset that grew enormously over that period. That is a real record and a genuinely encouraging one, and it is also a small sample by the standards of any other market.

The falls were severe. Every approach here, including the best one, spent time down more than 50% from its high. Anyone who sold during those stretches locked in the loss, which is why the honest advice is to size a position so that sitting through one is bearable.

Trading costs and tax are excluded from these figures. Nothing here predicts the future, and nobody, including anyone confidently telling you otherwise, knows what the price does next.
